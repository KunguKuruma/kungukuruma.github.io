Profile

I hold an MSc in Data Analytics from Dublin Business School and have worked with data across research, finance and operations. My dissertation benchmarked seven models for brain tumour classification from MRI and found that their errors were not spread evenly: glioma was the weakest class in every model, whatever the architecture. That finding is the subject of a journal article now under peer review, and it changed how I judge medical imaging models, by where and why they fail as much as by their headline scores.

Before this, at KALRO in Kenya, I supported KilimoSTAT, an agricultural data platform, integrating data from several institutions and preparing it for machine learning. That work gave me early experience of building data pipelines for applied AI outside a clean benchmark setting.

Research direction and fit with the MIA Lab

Diagnostic imaging models are usually reported with a single aggregate score, and that score hides a lot. In my MSc work, an InceptionV3 model with 86% accuracy recalled only 53% of gliomas, and the same class was the weakest in all seven models. A system that misses nearly half the cases of one tumour type is not clinically usable, however good its average. My thesis first framed this for Kenya's healthcare system, where diagnostic delays for brain tumours are a serious problem.

I want to work on making diagnostic models dependable outside the setting they were built in. Three lines of work connect my background to research in Dr. Ghassan Hamarneh's Medical Image Analysis Lab at Simon Fraser University:

  • Explainability that clinicians can use. Explanations only matter if they help a clinician decide. I want to generate and evaluate explanations for multimodal medical image models, which relates to the lab's work on post-hoc explanation and on the clinical utility of AI assistance and its explanation. My lung cancer proposal, which pairs CT imaging with clinical variables and builds explanation into the design, is a starting point.
  • Why errors concentrate, and knowing when a model is wrong. My MSc work showed errors gathering in one class at the glioma and meningioma boundary. I want to ask whether such failures come from visual overlap, annotation noise and disagreement, or model limits, and to build models that flag the cases they are likely to get wrong. The lab's work on annotation noise and dataset quality in dermatology is a natural place to study this.
  • Generalisation and fairness across sites and groups. Test whether performance holds across scanners, institutions and patient populations, and whether it holds fairly, which connects to the lab's work on generalisable continual classification and on mitigating bias in medical image analysis.

I would like to pursue these questions as a PhD student in Dr. Hamarneh's lab, with the aim of detection that changes clinical outcomes, not only benchmark rankings.

Research work

Brain tumour classification from MRI using deep learning

MSc dissertation · 2024–2025 · Dublin Business School
  • Benchmarked seven models: logistic regression, SVM, decision tree, a custom CNN, and three pretrained transfer learning architectures (VGG19, Xception, InceptionV3).
  • Built a custom CNN from scratch (dropout, global average pooling, ReLU) and compared 60, 80 and 200 epoch runs to characterise overfitting and convergence.
  • Fine-tuned the pretrained networks over 40 epochs with selective layer freezing and unfreezing to adapt them to MRI data.
  • Implemented a preprocessing pipeline with ROI cropping, resizing and augmentation to address class imbalance.
  • Chose recall as the primary clinical metric to limit false negatives, and documented failure modes through confusion matrix analysis.
Research proposal · 2026 · unpublished

Clinically deployable lung cancer detection system

Independent research · Irish healthcare context

A PhD-level proposal on the gap between high-performing experimental lung cancer models and real clinical deployment.

  • Proposed an end-to-end system: GDPR-compliant data ingestion, a structured database layer, standardised CT and PET-CT preprocessing, deep learning classification, embedded explainability, and a clinician-facing dashboard.
  • Proposed a multimodal architecture combining CT imaging with structured clinical variables (age, sex, smoking history).
  • From the literature: moderate pooled specificity (0.68) and limited cross-institutional robustness in current systems. Set specificity, false positive reduction and clinical trust as primary evaluation criteria alongside accuracy and AUC.
